What Is the Stop-Signal Task?

The stop-signal task is the standard laboratory measure of response cancellation — the ability to abort an action you've already started. The rules are simple to state and genuinely hard to do: most trials show a go stimulus (say, an arrow pointing left or right) that you respond to as fast as you can. On a minority of trials, a second signal — a tone, a color change — appears shortly after the go stimulus, telling you to withhold the response you were already launching. Whether you succeed depends on a race you can't see.

That race is the task's theoretical core. Logan and Cowan's 1984 race-model account treats each stop trial as a competition between two independent processes: the go process, triggered by the arrow, and the stop process, triggered by the signal. If the stop process finishes first, you hold back; if the go process wins, you tap anyway. By varying the delay between the go stimulus and the stop signal (the stop-signal delay, or SSD), researchers can estimate how fast your covert stop process runs — the famous stop-signal reaction time, which never produces an observable response of its own. The task has become the workhorse measure of inhibitory control in studies of ADHD, impulse control, and aging. As with other training paradigms, honest reporting matters: practicing response cancellation trains response cancellation in the task, and claims of broad real-world transfer remain contested in the literature.

What makes a real stop-signal implementation

A few details separate an honest stop-signal task from a lookalike:

  • A real race. The stop signal must arrive after the go stimulus, while the response is in flight — not simultaneously, which turns the task into plain go/no-go. The SSD relative to the response window determines how often stopping succeeds, and a late-enough signal is what creates genuine cancellation pressure.
  • Visible, correctly timed signals. Some apps make difficulty by shrinking the SSD until the signal is nearly invisible — that measures perception, not inhibition. A faithful implementation keeps the signal clearly detectable at a consistent point mid-trial and gets difficulty from the response window instead.
  • Planted trial-type frequencies. Stop trials should occur at a controlled rate (a quarter to a third of trials is common), not left to chance, with a cap on consecutive same-type trials so long runs of go trials can't lull you into autopilot — or long runs of stops can't make withholding trivial.
  • Failure modes that distinguish errors. A premature go (committing before the signal arrives) is a different error than tapping on the stop signal after it appears, and both differ from a wrong-direction tap or a go timeout. Implementations that score all of these as "wrong" throw away the data the paradigm exists to produce.

Stop signal in Avoken

Avoken's Shifting Signal workout is a hybrid of the classic stop-signal task and cued go/no-go: each rep shows a single left or right arrow that starts either black or red and may shift color after the SSD, producing four trial types — standard go, stop-signal (black turns red: cancel the in-flight tap), sustained no-go (red stays red), and cued go (red turns black: release from inhibition and tap). Because you can't predict the type at arrow onset, every rep requires watching for the shift. The six levels hold the SSD fixed at 50% of the go response window — the shift always lands mid-trial, clearly visible but late enough to force real cancellation — while the window itself shrinks and the red signal becomes less salient at higher levels. Trial types roll at flat per-rep probabilities (go 30%, stop-signal 30%, no-go 20%, cued-go 20%) with a cap of two consecutive reps of the same type, and timing is fixed per level rather than adaptive — under accessibility timing, both the window and the SSD scale together so the 50% race structure is preserved.

Avoken is a training app, not a treatment — we describe the task, and we don't claim it transfers anywhere else. If you like the paradigm, it's a careful implementation.
