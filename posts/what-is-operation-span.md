# What Is the Operation Span Task?

Operation span — and the broader family of "complex span" tasks it belongs
to — is the other standard way researchers measure working memory. Where the
N-back asks you to continuously match a stream of items, complex span asks
you to do two things at once: a processing task and a storage task,
interleaved. In the classic version (Turner & Engle 1989; automated by
Unsworth and colleagues in 2005) you see a short math equation, judge
whether it's true or false, then see a word or letter to hold in memory.
After several of these equation–item pairs, you recall all the stored items
in order. The math isn't decoration — it exists to block rehearsal, so the
items have to be maintained under genuine dual-task load.

The paradigm earned its place because it works as an instrument. Complex
span scores correlate reliably with fluid intelligence and reading
comprehension — more strongly than simple storage tasks do — which is why
the operation span became a fixture of working-memory research alongside
the N-back (Engle 2002). The honest caveat is the same one that applies to
the whole field: scores on the task predict performance on other measures,
but evidence that practicing the task transfers to anything beyond it is
thin. The task measures a capacity; whether training on it changes that
capacity is an open question.

## What separates a real operation span from a lookalike

A few details separate an honest complex span from a memory checklist with math
bolted on:

- **Interleaved processing and storage.** Each episode must pair a
  genuine processing judgment (verify an equation) with a to-be-remembered
  item — not math first, then a separate memory list. The interleaving is
  what prevents rehearsal, and rehearsal-prevention is the entire point.
- **Ordered recall.** Complex span tests serial recall: you must reproduce
  the items in presentation order, typically by selecting from a pool that
  includes distractors. Recognition alone ("was this word shown?") is a
  weaker, different task.
- **A bounded span.** Published complex-span work tops out around 5–7
  items per trial, consistent with known working-memory limits (Daneman &
  Carpenter; Cowan 2001). Apps that scale the list forever are measuring
  mnemonic strategies, not span.
- **A real processing demand.** The equations need a time window tight
  enough to actually interrupt maintenance, and hard enough to occupy
  attention. If the math is trivial or untimed, it's not loading working
  memory — it's a delay interval you can rehearse through.
- **Recall-time pressure.** Conway and colleagues' scoring conventions
  treat the recall window as part of the task: unlimited retrieval time
  invites reconstruction strategies that inflate scores.

## Operation span in Avoken

Avoken ships an operation-span workout called OpSpan that follows the
Unsworth-style automated structure. Each repetition runs a sequence of
episodes — a true/false equation to verify within a processing window,
followed by a word to hold — and then presents a shuffled recall pool of
the target words plus same-category distractors. You tap the words in the
order they appeared; any wrong word or order fails the rep, and equation
answers give immediate green/red feedback but don't count toward the
score — the recall is the measure, matching the published convention.

Across its eight levels, the workout moves the axes the literature uses:
span grows from 2 to a hard cap of 5 items (the published complex-span
ceiling), the processing window tightens from 4000 to 2500ms and the
storage window from 2000 to 1200ms, equations progress from single-digit
addition to multi-step operations, and the distractor count grows from 2
to 6. A dimmed decoy word list can flash before recall — a published
interference wrinkle the instructions warn you to ignore. Session presets
run fewer, longer reps than most Avoken workouts, since each rep is a
full encode–recall cycle.
